# Workbench editorial guide

Write for a colleague deciding whether to try an activity and a skeptical reader deciding whether to trust its account. Offer a concrete task to try and a concrete observation from trying it, where one is available. Identify proposals as proposals.

## Evidence and confidence

Keep instructions (what students were asked to do), observations (what actually happened), and rationale (why an activity might help) distinct. Use “Learning aims” for intended outcomes and “Teaching rationale” for an unevaluated explanation. Completing a task does not establish learning. Do not manufacture a “What happened” section without observations; name the specific gap instead.

Keep the original handout linked. Label proposed revisions so readers can distinguish them from the activity as run. Do not invent student reactions, instructor observations, or testing histories.

Limit technical explanations to what the activity establishes. Different outputs do not establish personalization or its cause. An output cannot reveal a training corpus. A model's account of its process is another claim to check. A failed search or archival silence does not prove something never existed.

## Practical detail and examples

Use existing front matter and typography includes. Do not add metadata fields that do not render. Make these details easy to find in the body:

- prior reading or subject knowledge;
- materials, access, and instructor preparation;
- the order of tasks and realistic time or scope;
- student submissions and criteria for a defensible response;
- a practical difficulty and possible adaptation.

Name required tool capabilities. State that a free account suffices only when checked. Where appropriate, offer instructor-supplied output as an adaptation.

Include one short worked example showing the intellectual decision, rather than only a prompt. Prefer an actual example when the record and permission to share support it. Otherwise label it **Constructed example** and identify invented passages or records. It must not appear to be student work or recorded AI output. Never invent quotations from a reading or archival collection. Label new instructions **Suggested adaptation** if they change the activity as run.

Do not require an error, discovery, or benefit. Accurate output, unresolved evidence, or an unhelpful exchange can support a good submission. Explain what evidence is needed to accept a claim as well as reject it.

## Voice and length

Use concrete verbs and objects: quote a passage, compare claims, locate a source, revise an objection. Replace broad promises about critical thinking with the decision the student must make.

State the central idea once, then develop it through instructions, evidence, or an example. Cut repeated contrasts between finishing and learning, fluency and accuracy, or generating and judging. Avoid slogans, inevitable claims about student AI use, and conclusions that repeat the introduction.

Use first person for actual decisions and experience. Keep useful disciplinary language but explain what terms such as “silence” or “method” require students to notice. Length follows the activity: cut repeated rationale before instructions and examples. Sections are optional; do not force every sketch into the same structure.

## Status labels

Status describes experience and revision, not proven learning or guaranteed readiness for another course:

- **rough:** an initial proposal or account with significant questions or practical details unresolved; it may have been tried.
- **lightly tested:** tried in a limited setting, with little documented follow-up.
- **tested:** enough experience is recorded to describe practical findings and limitations.
- **refined:** revised in response to use, with changes and remaining limitations documented.

Only change a label when the record supports it. A prose edit or constructed example does not make an activity tested. If evidence for a current label is missing, identify the gap rather than inventing a testing history. Context and last-run fields establish provenance, not effectiveness.

## Editorial review

Can a reader identify the task and submission? Is the example honestly labeled? Are observations distinct from hopes and proposed revisions? Can students report that the tool was unhelpful? Are technical claims supported? Does each paragraph add something the reader needs?
